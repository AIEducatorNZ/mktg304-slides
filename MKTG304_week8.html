Strategic framework

The strategic hierarchy

Goal
The broad business ambition (given to you)
A high-level statement of what the organisation is trying to achieve. Not time-bound, not directly measurable. Music Planet gives you three.
↓
Objective
A SMART, measurable outcome (you generate these)
Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Each objective serves a goal, and a strong one can serve more than one. This is what gets measured.
↓
Tactic
The specific digital marketing activity (you generate these)
The action taken to achieve the objective. Channel-specific, actionable, measurable. One tactic can support more than one objective.
↓
KPI · KSF
How you measure it, and what must be true (you generate these)
KPIs measure whether the tactic is working. KSFs are the preconditions that must be in place for it to work at all.
Music Planet hands you the three goals. Everything below the top line, you build today. That is the work of Assignment 3, and the reason this session is generative rather than a lecture.
The hierarchy is the structural foundation for the session and for Assignment 3. The important shift this year: students are not handed objectives and tactics. They are given three goals and generate the rest. Emphasise two properties that pay off later. First, an objective must be SMART, so you can answer did we achieve it with data at the end of the campaign. Students frequently offer vague tactic-shaped goals like increase our social presence, which is neither an objective nor measurable. Second, the hierarchy is not strictly one-to-one: a well-chosen objective can serve more than one goal, and a well-chosen tactic can serve more than one objective. That overlap is where budget efficiency comes from, and it is the analytical prize in Section 2. Do not rush this slide, the whole cascade depends on the vocabulary being clear.

Students' instinct is to dump everything, the whole audit, every persona, all the competitor data, on the theory that more is better. The real skill is curation: signal, not volume, since irrelevant detail pulls the model off-target as badly as thin context does. Do not hand them the list. Keep the menu framing: several candidates per goal, then a reasoned selection, not one right answer.
From objectives to tactics

Generating tactics: clear the bar, then earn double

The bar: it has to be new
Music Planet already runs paid ads, organic social, email, and SEO. Proposing that they do those things is not a proposal, it is a description of their status quo with a budget attached. Every tactic must be new, novel, or a significant and evidenced improvement on what they already do. Your A2 audit documents their current activity, so check it: if they already do it, it is not a tactic. SEO fails this bar twice, it is too slow to move in 30 days, and it is already in place.
Then earn double: hunt for overlap
A $10,000 budget over 30 days is tight. The strongest proposals find tactics that serve more than one objective, and objectives that serve more than one goal. One well-chosen activity earns its place twice. Design for overlap, do not just notice it after the fact.
Illustrative overlap
Tactic: a short-form video series featuring in-store staff demoing gear
↓  serves  ↓
Engagement objective
Awareness objective
↓  which lift  ↓
Two goals at once, from one production cost
Generate freely, then gate hard: reject anything Music Planet already does unless you can make it meaningfully better, and prefer what pulls double duty.
Two rules govern good tactic generation, and this slide leads with the more important one. The bar comes first because it is a gate every tactic must pass: Music Planet already runs paid, organic social, email, and SEO, so a tactic that simply repeats their current activity is not a proposal, it is their status quo with a price tag. The standard is new, novel, or a significant and evidenced improvement on what they already do, and the evidence for what they already do is the students' own A2 audit. This is the single most common weakness in campaign proposals, students proposing run Google Ads to a client already running Google Ads. SEO is the clearest case of failing the bar: it fails twice, too slow to move inside a 30-day window, and already in place, which is why it belongs in strategic recommendations rather than campaign tactics, covered later. The second rule is the efficiency move, overlap: with only $10,000 over 30 days, a tactic that serves two objectives, or an objective that ladders into two goals, effectively doubles the return. The staff-demo video example shows the shape. In the exercise that follows, grade both hard: reject status-quo tactics, and reward genuine cross-coverage.

KPIs and measurement

A KPI is the instrument that reads a SMART objective

They share the same numeric, time-bound DNA, because they describe the same target from two ends. So what makes a KPI a distinct thing, not just a restated objective?
The SMART objective
"Grow online sales of starter kits by 15% within the 30-day campaign"
The commitment. It ladders up to a goal (online sales). It states what you are trying to achieve.
Specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound. It does not say how you would read it, or which activity produces it.
The KPI that reads it
"Completed checkouts on the starter-kit page, from GA4, tracked weekly against target"
The instrument. It ladders down to a specific tactic. It is what you actually read to see whether the objective is being hit.
Adds the two things the objective leaves out, below.
1. It names its tool
The objective never says "measured in GA4". A KPI must. "Track performance" is not a tool.
2. It attaches to a tactic
An objective ladders up to a goal. A KPI ladders down to the activity you are running. No tactic named, not a campaign KPI.
The overlap with SMART is real and fine. When you check your KPIs in Exercise 3, do not just re-test SMART. Test the two things that make a KPI an instrument: does it name a tool, and does it attach to a specific tactic?
This slide exists because of a good student question: is not this just a SMART objective again? The honest answer is that a KPI and its objective share numeric, time-bound DNA because they describe the same target from two ends, so three of the usual quality checks, measurable, has a target, time-bound, are indeed just SMART restated. Rather than hide that overlap, make it the teaching point. The objective is the commitment and ladders up to a goal. The KPI is the instrument you read to see whether the commitment is being met, and it ladders down to a specific tactic. What a KPI adds, and what an objective never contains, is exactly two things: it names the measurement tool, and it attaches to a named tactic. Those are the two checks that distinguish a KPI from a rephrased objective, and they are the two students most often omit, they write a measurable, time-bound line and forget to say GA4 or Meta Ads Manager, and forget which tactic it measures. In Exercise 3, do not let students simply re-run SMART on their KPIs, push them on tool and tactic. The starter-kit example is deliberately the same target expressed as an objective on the left and a KPI on the right, so the shared DNA and the two additions are visible side by side. One aside worth making if it comes up: this also quietly explains why SEO rankings fail as a campaign KPI, the tool and tactic are fine, but the target cannot move within 30 days, which is the objective side of the pair breaking, not the instrument side.
KPIs and measurement

KPIs vs KSFs. They are not the same

KPI · Key Performance Indicator
A metric that measures progress toward an objective
Answers: "How are we performing?"
Music Planet examples
  • Online store sessions up 20% month on month
  • Add-to-cart to checkout conversion rate 3.5%
  • Cost per online sale under $40
  • Branded search volume for "Music Planet" up 15%
KSF · Key Success Factor
A condition that must be in place for a tactic to succeed
Answers: "What has to be true for this to work?"
Music Planet examples
  • Product pages load in under 2 seconds on mobile
  • Consistent posting cadence, at least two posts per week
  • Ad creative honours the brand voice, grounded not corporate
  • GA4 ecommerce and conversion tracking correctly configured
Both appear in Assignment 3, and they are not interchangeable. A KPI is measured after the fact. A KSF is a precondition that must be in place before the tactic can work.
This distinction trips up many students. A common error: listing good content quality as a KPI when it is actually a KSF. You cannot measure good content quality as a performance indicator, it is a condition you either meet or do not before launch. Walk through one of each. Online store sessions up 20% month on month is a KPI because you can check it with data afterwards. A consistent posting cadence of at least two posts per week is a KSF, because if it is not in place the social tactic cannot work regardless of how good the content is. Note the Music Planet flavour: honouring the brand voice, grounded and not corporate, is a genuine KSF for their creative, straight from the brief. Ask students to spot a KSF they may have mislabelled as a KPI in their own audit.
KPIs and measurement

Benchmarks: why they matter for Assignment 3

What a benchmark is
An industry or channel reference point that tells you what good performance looks like for your tactic and channel. Benchmarks give your predictions credibility.
The principle
A prediction without a benchmark is a guess.
If you predict a 3.5% conversion rate, you need to explain why 3.5% is reasonable. Without a benchmark, the number has no analytical foundation.
How to find benchmarks
Search for benchmarks specific to your tactics and channels. Major ad platforms, email providers, and marketing research firms publish these. Be specific: a retail or ecommerce conversion-rate benchmark beats a generic website average, and a music or specialty-retail figure beats a generic ecommerce one.
In Assignment 3: every predicted KPI needs a benchmark source. State the benchmark, its source, and why it applies to Music Planet. Benchmark sources belong in your reference list.
Benchmarks are required in Assignment 3 but students frequently omit them or use them vaguely, industry average with no source. The teaching point is specificity: a conversion-rate benchmark for a specialty music retailer selling to NZ consumers is a different number from a generic ecommerce average, which is different again from a B2B SaaS figure. Students should hunt for benchmarks as close to Music Planet's context as they can find, and be transparent when they are forced to use a broader proxy. Where to find them: major ad platforms publish quarterly or annual benchmark reports for paid search and paid social, email platforms publish email benchmarks, and research firms publish sector reports. Students should search for these directly rather than relying on a single tool or a number from a slide.

Connecting your audit

From audit evidence to defensible tactics Worked example

The discipline
Your campaign does not start from scratch. Every major strategic choice should trace back to something you learned in the audit. Where the audit cannot tell you enough, state what must be verified before recommending action.
StepContent
Audit finding Semrush showed Music Planet ranks on page one organically for "beginner guitar NZ" but does not appear in the paid results for that same high-intent term, while Rockshop and MusicWorks both run ads on it.
Verify first Confirm in Music Planet's Google Ads account whether that term is genuinely unbid, or bid but losing impression share. You cannot see this from outside, so flag it as an assumption to check.
Strategic implication If the term is unbid, competitors are capturing high-intent clicks above Music Planet's strong organic listing. A small paid presence could defend that ground for the campaign period.
Conditional tactic If verified, run a tightly scoped Google Ads campaign on high-intent transactional terms where Music Planet has organic strength but no paid presence, pointing to the matching product pages.
KPIs Impression share on the target terms (Google Ads) · Conversion rate from those ads (GA4 ecommerce)
KSF The destination product pages load in under 2 seconds on mobile and match the search intent of the term
Use this sequence in Assignment 3: audit finding → verify → implication → conditional tactic → KPI/KSF. The tactic is proposed if verified, not asserted blind.
This slide now does the work that the previous bridge slide and worked example were doing separately. Do not re-teach the whole audit-to-strategy relationship here; that has already been established through the exercises. The new emphasis is intellectual honesty. Every major strategic choice should trace to audit evidence, but when the evidence does not let students see inside Music Planet's accounts, they should state what would need to be verified first. Walk through the sequence: audit finding, verify first, implication, conditional tactic, KPIs and KSF. The search example is deliberately conditional because Music Planet already runs Google Ads and SEO; students cannot responsibly recommend starting from scratch as if it is greenfield. Reward students who say what they would check before recommending spend, rather than pretending they know what the account already contains.
Connecting your audit

How each audit section feeds into your campaign

Some audit findings lead to campaign tactics. Others lead to strategic recommendations that sit outside the 30-day campaign.
Audit sectionWhat it informs in your proposal
Website audit Campaign tactics. Conversion rate and UX improvements have fast, measurable impact within the campaign window. Product-page and checkout quality directly affect paid and organic conversion.
Paid advertising audit Campaign tactics. Channel selection, ad creative, offers, and budget allocation flow from what the audit found about current paid performance and gaps, including where competitors outbid Music Planet.
Social media audit Campaign tactics. Content, community activity, and organic social can be executed and measured within the window. Competitor gaps against Rockshop and MusicWorks inform what Music Planet should test.
SEO audit Mostly strategic recommendations, not campaign tactics, with one exception. See callout below.
SEO ranking work belongs in your strategic recommendations, not your campaign tactics. Meaningful ranking gains take three to six months, longer than a 30-day campaign. The one thing an SEO finding can do inside the campaign is reveal a paid gap: where Music Planet ranks organically but competitors run ads, a conditional paid tactic can defend that ground now, while genuine ranking improvement stays a longer-term recommendation.
One of the most practically important slides for Assignment 3. The SEO callout addresses the most common structural error: students proposing SEO as a primary campaign tactic with a KPI like rank on page one for three keywords in six weeks, which is unrealistic on a 30-day timeline. The correct treatment is to acknowledge SEO gaps from the audit, put ranking work in strategic recommendations, and focus campaign budget on tactics that produce measurable results inside the window. The nuance to reinforce, and the one that ties the whole search thread together: an SEO finding is still useful inside the campaign when it exposes a paid gap, as in the Slide 14 example, where strong organic and absent paid on the same term justifies a conditional paid tactic. That is the SEO and PPC interweave, ranking improvement is long-term, but the gap between organic and paid is actionable now, once verified.

Campaign structure

Budget framing: $10,000 over 30 days

Principle 1
Budget your tactics, grouped by objective
Money is spent on tactics, so cost each one. Group those costs under the objective they serve, and give higher-priority objectives a bigger share of the $10,000. A tactic serving two objectives is costed once.
Principle 2
Every tactic needs a predicted return
Use benchmarks to estimate what the spend should produce. If you cannot estimate a return, you cannot justify the allocation.
Principle 3
Justify, do not guess
A budget with no rationale loses marks. Every allocation should reference a benchmark, a cost estimate, or a comparable outcome. Overlapping tactics stretch the $10k further.
Example budget table structure, replace placeholders with your own figures. Total must sum to $10,000:
TacticChannelBudget allocationPredicted metricBenchmark source
Paid social awareness campaign Meta (Facebook & Instagram) $X of $10,000 X impressions at $X CPM [Industry benchmark report]
High-intent search defence Google Ads $X of $10,000 X clicks at $X CPC [Platform benchmark data]
Staff-demo video & landing pages Organic social / owned $X (production cost) X engaged sessions in the window [Semrush / GA4 baseline]
Budget framing is often the weakest section of student proposals. The three principles address the three most common errors: costing at the wrong level or with arbitrary splits, allocations with no predicted return, and guesses dressed as analysis. Principle 1 is the one to be precise about: money is spent on tactics, not objectives, so students cost each tactic, then group those costs under the objective it serves, and give higher-priority objectives a larger share of the $10,000. The objective is the grouping and the priority lens, the tactic is where the dollars actually attach. This also resolves the overlap question from Section 2 cleanly: a tactic serving two objectives is costed once, not double-counted, and simply sits under both. The example table is intentionally incomplete, students build their own with real figures, and the total must sum to $10,000 over 30 days. The benchmark source column is critical, it forces students to attribute predicted metrics to a real reference point. Walk through the example and ask what would go in the benchmark source column for a Meta awareness campaign, and where they would find that data, this prompts the research they need to do before finalising the proposal.

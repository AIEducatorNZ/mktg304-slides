Section 1 · Google Display Network

Google Display Network

Reach, targeting, bidding, and campaign design for visual advertising across the web.
We start with the Display Network, then move to Search PPC in the second half. The GDN is often underestimated as a channel: students tend to think of it as banner ads. The reality is a sophisticated, AI-powered system for reaching audiences across millions of placements. For Music Planet this is the channel most relevant to their awareness goal, which is exactly why the awareness tension surfaces here first. Start with the fundamentals.

The Google Display Network

What it is
  • Millions of websites, apps, and video platforms
  • Reaches 90%+ of internet users globally
  • Ads appear while people browse, not while they search
  • Formats: image, responsive display, video, rich media
Demand Gen: the extended reach
Demand Gen campaigns extend display-style ads across YouTube, Discover, and Gmail. Part of Google's "Power Pack" of AI-driven campaign types alongside Performance Max and AI Max.
Key distinction from Search: Display is passive. Your ad appears while someone reads an article or uses an app, not because they searched for your product. That makes it powerful for brand awareness and remarketing, which is precisely the ground Music Planet's awareness goal lives on.
The GDN is Google's network for placing visual ads across the web. The key distinction from search is that display is passive: your ad appears while someone reads an article or uses an app, not because they searched for your product. This makes it powerful for brand awareness and remarketing. The formats are visual: banner images, responsive ads that adapt to different placements, video, and interactive rich media. One development worth knowing is Demand Gen, Google's newer campaign type that pushes display-style creative across YouTube, Discover feeds, and Gmail. It sits alongside Performance Max and AI Max in what Google calls the Power Pack. Think of Demand Gen as the GDN's reach extended into Google's owned surfaces. For Music Planet, the passive nature of display is a double-edged sword: it is the natural home for awareness, but it is also exactly the kind of top-of-funnel activity the client says has burned budget before, so hold that thought for Practice 1.

Match your bidding strategy to your campaign goal. The choice flows from what you are trying to achieve, not from what is cheapest. For Music Planet the CPM path serves goal number one (awareness), but given their ROI focus, only propose it if you can tie that reach to a measurable next step.
CPC and CPM are two of several bidding options. The principle, matching how you pay to your goal, is what matters here. See Google's full list of Display bidding strategies.
Two main bidding options on the GDN. CPC means you only pay when someone clicks, which is ideal when you want measurable actions like online sales. CPM means you pay per thousand impressions regardless of clicks, ideal when the goal is getting your brand in front of as many relevant people as possible. The choice flows from your goal. For Music Planet, CPM maps to the awareness goal: reaching younger players and bedroom producers who currently default to global sites and do not know Music Planet has showrooms here. CPC maps to the online-sales goal: capturing people already researching a purchase. The teaching hook is the client's ROI stance. A pure CPM impressions campaign is exactly what they said wasted money, so if a team proposes CPM they must pair it with remarketing audiences and view-through measurement so the reach is not a dead end. We work that through next.

For Music Planet, custom segments built from searches like "beginner guitar NZ" or from the URLs of gear-review sites and forums are a strong way to reach genuine intent, well before anyone types the brand name.
Targeting on the GDN falls into two broad categories: audience-based and contextual. Audience targeting is about who you want to reach. Demographics are straightforward. Affinity audiences capture long-term interests, for example people passionate about music production. In-market audiences are people actively researching a purchase right now. Custom segments let you define your own audience using keywords people have searched, URLs they have visited, or apps they use. Remarketing re-engages people who have already been to the site, which for Music Planet is the bridge that makes awareness accountable. Contextual targeting is about where your ad appears: pages about certain topics, containing certain keywords, or specific sites you choose. Under advanced options, optimised targeting is Google's AI-driven expansion tool: you feed it your conversion data and first-party audiences as signals, and it finds additional users likely to convert. This replaced Similar Audiences, which Google removed in 2023, and reflects the broader shift to AI-driven targeting powered by first-party data rather than third-party cookies. A note on brand safety from the Music Planet brief: using competitor URLs as a custom-segment signal is a legitimate targeting technique, but the creative itself must not name or knock competitors. Keep that line clear for the mockup exercises.
Practice #1 · GDN

The awareness tension

Scenario
Music Planet's number one goal is brand awareness among younger players and bedroom producers who currently default to global sites. But the brief says past awareness campaigns underperformed on ROI, so the client now runs conversion-first. Here is the catch: brand awareness is genuinely hard to measure, which makes its ROI hard to prove. Illustrative test allocation: $1,000 of the $10,000.
Your questions
  1. When they say awareness "hasn't worked," is that an awareness problem or a measurement problem? How would you tell?
  2. Do they even have the tracking to link awareness to a sale? If not, what goes in place first?
  3. If last-click ROI cannot capture awareness, what would you measure instead?
  4. How would you convince an ROI-first client that awareness now drives sales later, and what evidence would you bring?
The crux
"It didn't work" and "we never measured it properly" look identical on a conversion report. Separate those two first. Then make the harder case: that awareness today becomes revenue later, to a client who counts only last-click sales.
This exercise is deliberately uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the point. Push students onto the measurement problem, because that is where most of them will not have thought hard. Start with the trap in the client's own words. When Music Planet says awareness "hasn't worked," that claim is doing a lot of quiet work. It could mean the activity genuinely failed to move anything. Or it could mean they ran it, judged it on a last-click conversion report, saw few immediate sales, and concluded it failed, when in truth they never measured awareness at all. Those two situations look identical on a standard conversions dashboard and call for opposite responses. Get students to sit with that ambiguity before they propose a campaign. Then the capability question: do they even have the instrumentation? Linking awareness to a sale needs more than a conversion pixel. It needs some mix of brand-lift measurement, branded-search and direct-traffic baselines, remarketing audiences so exposed users can be followed, view-through and assisted-conversion tracking, and ideally a geo or matched-market holdout comparing exposed against unexposed. If none of that exists, the first honest recommendation is not a campaign, it is putting measurement in place. What to measure instead of last-click sales: branded search volume lift, direct and organic traffic lift, growth of remarketing and customer-match lists, engaged-visit rate, view-through and assisted conversions, and repeat or downstream purchase from exposed cohorts. Impressions and raw reach alone are the vanity trap to avoid. The hardest question is the awareness-to-sales link, and this is where you can bring in theory if the group is ready. The core argument is that at any given moment only a small share of the category is ready to buy, so reaching the much larger not-yet-in-market group builds the memory that pays off when they do enter. This is the mental-availability idea from the Ehrenberg-Bass tradition, and the long-versus-short evidence from Binet and Field that brand building compounds over longer horizons than short-term activation. I would keep those names off the slide, but they are useful scaffolding when a student asks "says who?". If anyone cites specific figures, tell them to verify against the primary source. There is no single right answer. Reward students who separate the measurement problem from the awareness problem, who are honest about whether the tools even exist yet, and who can make a credible, evidenced case for delayed payback rather than just asserting that awareness matters.

Practice #2b · GDN

ROAS for a retailer

ROAS = Revenue from advertising ÷ Cost of advertising
The retail twist, part 1: margin
A $180 order is not $180 of profit. At a blended gross margin around 35%, that order is roughly $63 of gross profit.
The retail twist, part 2: repeat purchase
Consumables (strings, sticks, cables) bring players back. One customer is worth more than one order.
Illustrative figures, not actual Music Planet data. Average order value: $180. Blended gross margin: 35%. An engaged customer makes about 5 purchases over roughly 2 years, so retail CLV is about $900 in revenue, or about $315 in gross profit.
Your calculations (using Practice #2a results)
  1. Calculate first-purchase revenue ROAS
  2. Adjust for margin: calculate gross-profit ROAS
  3. Calculate CLV-based ROAS
  4. Does the campaign clear the 4:1 benchmark, and on which measure?
This is where the reworking from a subscription business to a retailer really lands, and it is the most important analytical point of the day. A one-off ROAS number can flatter or mislead a retailer in two ways. Working the maths from Practice 2a (40 sales, $7,200 revenue, $1,000 spend, all illustrative): first-purchase revenue ROAS is $7,200 divided by $1,000, which is 7.2:1. That looks great. But revenue is not profit. Apply the illustrative 35% gross margin and the gross profit is about $2,520, so the gross-profit ROAS is roughly 2.5:1. That is the number a finance-minded client cares about, and it is well below what the headline revenue figure implied. Now the other direction: a musician does not buy once. With an illustrative CLV of about $900 in revenue per acquired customer, 40 customers are worth about $36,000 over their relationship, so CLV-based revenue ROAS is about 36:1; on gross profit (about $315 per customer, so about $12,600) it is closer to 12.6:1. The teaching point: the 4:1 rule of thumb is a revenue heuristic built for one-off e-commerce. For a retailer like Music Planet you should not read it literally off first-purchase revenue. Margin drags the true return down; repeat purchase pulls the justified acquisition spend up. The right question is whether your cost to acquire a customer sits comfortably below their gross-profit CLV. Reward students who reach that framing rather than just reciting 4:1. Reiterate the numbers are illustrative throughout.

Google Search

Search PPC, SEO, bidding strategies, and Responsive Search Ads. The channel where Music Planet already competes head-to-head against nationwide chains.
Now we move to Google Search. Unlike a business that avoids search, Music Planet already runs Google Ads and actively manages SEO, and they compete directly against nationwide chains and global sites in the results page. So search is fully in play for Assignment 3, no channel is off the table. The mental model shift from Part 1 is intent: on search, people tell you what they want by typing it, which changes both targeting and how you think about awareness versus conversion.

Use both. PPC for now, SEO for later. Music Planet already does: the brief lists active Google Ads and actively managed SEO. For a 30-day campaign, PPC does the heavy lifting, while SEO is the compounding asset that keeps working after the budget stops.
Quick comparison before the Search PPC exercises. The fundamental trade-off is speed versus sustainability. PPC gives immediate visibility but costs per click. SEO takes months to build but delivers sustainable organic traffic. Most effective strategies use both, and Music Planet already does: their brief lists Google Ads and actively managed SEO as current activity. Two developments to note. First, Google now shows ads within AI Overviews, the AI-generated summaries at the top of results, which is a new surface for PPC. Second, AI Overviews can reduce organic click-through for some informational queries, which changes the SEO calculus. For a 30-day campaign the practical point is that PPC is what can move inside the window; SEO is a longer investment you would recommend as a parallel track, not something that pays off within the month. Note explicitly for A3: because search is a live channel for Music Planet, Search PPC and SEO are both fair game in proposals, unlike a client who excludes search.

Automated

The direction of travel: AI Max
Launched 2025. Broader keyword matching, dynamic URLs, automated creative assembly from your headline and description assets. The trend: advertisers provide strategic signals; AI handles execution. The quality of your inputs matters more than ever.
Bidding on Search PPC ranges from fully manual to fully automated. Manual CPC gives complete control: you decide what each keyword is worth. Enhanced CPC was the bridge between manual and automated, adjusting bids to chase conversions, but Google is deprecating it in favour of fully automated options. On the automated side, Maximise Clicks is the simplest: spend the budget getting the most clicks. Target CPA and Target ROAS are smarter because they optimise toward business outcomes, not just traffic; for an ROI-focused client like Music Planet, Target CPA or Target ROAS align naturally with how they think. Maximise Conversions is similar but without a specific cost target. The bigger picture is AI Max, which launched in 2025 and represents Google's vision for search advertising: less reliance on manual keyword lists, broader AI-powered matching, and automated creative assembly from your assets. The trend is clear: advertisers provide the strategic inputs and the AI handles execution. That does not make the human role less important; it makes input quality more important. Weak conversion tracking, thin creative, and unclear objectives get amplified by AI just as much as good ones do.

Google Ads in 2026: what is changing

AI is the engine
Power Pack: AI Max, Performance Max, and Demand Gen work together to drive campaign delivery. Advertisers provide signals; AI handles execution.
Ads in AI Overviews
Paid results now appear inside AI-generated search summaries at the top of results. A fundamentally new surface for PPC, changing where and how ads are seen.
Legacy formats retiring
Expanded Text Ads gone (2022). Call-only ads: new campaigns blocked Feb 2026, serving ends 2026 to 2027. The direction is flexible, asset-based formats that AI assembles.
First-party data is critical
Similar Audiences removed (2023). Third-party cookies are phasing out. Targeting now relies on your own customer data and conversion signals as inputs to Google's AI.
For your A3 proposals: search is fully in play for Music Planet, and so is display. The strategic question in 2026 is less "which keywords" and more "what signals and assets will you feed Google's AI," and how you keep an awareness-first goal accountable to an ROI-first client.
Four things shaping Google Ads right now. First, AI is no longer an add-on; it is the core of how campaigns run. Google's Power Pack gives three AI-driven campaign types that work together: Demand Gen for awareness, AI Max for search intent, Performance Max for full-funnel scale. Second, ads now appear inside AI Overviews: when Google generates an AI summary at the top of results, ads can show within it, a fundamentally new surface. Third, Google is systematically retiring legacy ad formats. Expanded Text Ads went in 2022; call-only ads cannot be created from February 2026 and stop serving by 2027. The direction is toward flexible, asset-based formats the AI assembles. Fourth, first-party data matters more than ever: with Similar Audiences gone and third-party cookies phasing out, your own customer data and conversion signals power Google's automated targeting. A note on these dates and product claims: several of them, especially the February 2026 call-only change, sit at or beyond my reliable knowledge, so treat them as last semester's stated facts and worth a quick check against Google's current documentation before class. For A3, the takeaway is that both search and display are open channels for Music Planet, and the modern job is feeding good signals and assets to the AI while holding the awareness-versus-ROI tension we opened with.

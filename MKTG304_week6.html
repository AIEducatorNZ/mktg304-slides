Influencer marketing

What is influencer marketing?

Influencer marketing is the practice of partnering with individuals who have an established audience and credibility in a niche to promote a brand, product, or service.
US$16.4B → ~US$32.55B
Estimated global industry size, 2022 to 2025: the market has nearly doubled in three years. Different data providers calculate the annual growth rate differently (estimates from industry sources range roughly 25 to 35 percent), because they scope the market slightly differently.
Unlike celebrity endorsement
Built on perceived authenticity and audience trust, not fame. The influencer has a direct, ongoing relationship with their audience.
Unlike paid advertising
The message is delivered through the creator's own voice and format, not a brand-controlled placement.
The core mechanism
Borrowed credibility. The audience trusts the influencer; that trust transfers to the brand.
The borrowed credibility framing is the most important concept on this slide. Students often describe influencer marketing as "when someone with followers promotes a product," which is accurate but not analytical. The mechanism matters: why does it work? Because the influencer has established trust with their audience, and the act of endorsement transfers a portion of that trust to the brand. This is why authenticity is so commercially valuable, and why forced or over-scripted partnerships tend to underperform. Ask students: "What makes an endorsement feel authentic versus commercial?" before advancing. The scale stat is new for this year and worth 30 seconds: the near-doubling from US$16.4B to roughly US$32.55B (Influencer Marketing Hub's benchmark series, 2022 to 2025) establishes that this is now a material, fast-growing media channel, not a niche tactic. The growth-rate hedge is deliberate and should be said aloud: different industry sources (Influencer Marketing Hub, Statista, IAB) calculate annual growth differently because they scope "influencer marketing" differently, some include only direct influencer fees, others include broader creator advertising or platform revenue. That definitional inconsistency becomes directly relevant again on the market-size-by-country slide immediately after this one, so it is worth planting the seed here.

Influencer marketing

The influencer landscape

Tier Follower range Key characteristics
Nano 1K–10K High engagement, niche audiences, low cost, often willing to work for product
Micro 10K–100K Strong engagement, more defined niche, cost-effective for targeted campaigns
Macro 100K–1M Broader reach, more professional, higher cost, engagement begins to dilute
Mega / Celebrity 1M+ Mass reach, lowest engagement rates, highest cost and brand risk
Key pattern: Follower count changes scale, not automatically effectiveness. Nano and micro creators often lead on engagement rate, but the relationship is not a strict decline at every tier, and exact figures vary widely by platform and source.
The follower count benchmarks on this slide are widely used but not universal; they vary by platform and source. Frame them as useful working approximations rather than definitive thresholds. Be careful with the trade-off framing this year. The old version of this slide said engagement declines in a straight line as reach increases, and that overstates what the evidence actually shows. The genuinely well-supported pattern is a small-creator engagement advantage: nano and micro creators very consistently outperform larger tiers on engagement rate, across the sources checked for this deck. What is not well supported is a smooth, monotonic decline all the way up the tier ladder; several sources show the relationship flattening or behaving unevenly at the macro and mega end, and different providers report meaningfully different exact percentages for the same tiers on the same platform, sometimes by a factor of two or more. Do not put a specific engagement-rate-by-tier table on this slide or state one aloud as if it were settled; the honest version of this lesson is "small creators usually engage their audience more, exact numbers are contested, so evaluate the actual candidate rather than assuming from tier." Ask students: "For a brand like Music Planet targeting musicians and music hobbyists, what tier would you expect to perform best and why?" Hold the answer until Slide 10.
Influencer marketing

Selecting the right influencer

Three selection criteria
  • Audience fit: does their audience match your target segment?
  • Content alignment: does their tone and content style suit the brand?
  • Credibility signals: do they have a genuine relationship with their audience?
Four KPIs to evaluate before partnering
  • Engagement rate: likes + comments / followers, benchmarked by tier
  • Audience demographics: age, location, gender, via platform analytics or influencer tools
  • Follower quality: ratio of real vs bot or inactive accounts
  • Brand-safe content history: past partnerships, controversial posts, values alignment
The three criteria are a qualitative filter; the four KPIs are a quantitative filter. In practice, good influencer selection uses both. The qualitative criteria come first: if the audience fit is wrong, no engagement rate will compensate. The most common selection error students make in case studies is optimising for reach or follower count without checking audience fit. Emphasise: a micro-influencer with 15,000 followers who speaks directly to New Zealand gigging musicians and home-studio hobbyists is worth more to Music Planet than a macro-influencer with 400,000 followers whose audience is broad lifestyle consumers. On follower quality: bot accounts are a significant problem in influencer marketing. Tools like HypeAuditor or Modash can surface the estimated ratio of real vs fake followers. Students should know this check exists even if they haven't run it themselves.
Influencer marketing

Structuring the collaboration

Key elements of a brief
  • Campaign goals
  • Key messages
  • Deliverables: post type, quantity, timeline
  • Usage rights
  • Exclusivity clauses
Too much control kills authenticity. Too little risks off-brand content. The brief should direct without over-scripting.
Disclosure obligations in New Zealand
Influencer content is an ad if there is payment of any kind, financial or otherwise. ASA NZ's rule: it must be obvious to consumers when they first see it, not buried in a bio or revealed only after they interact with it. Accepted labels include "Ad," "Advert," "Advertisement," and "Sponsored content." Non-disclosure can also breach the Fair Trading Act.
The brief structure is standard across most campaign types, but influencer briefs have a unique tension that other formats do not: the more you control the creative, the less it sounds like the influencer. Students often assume the brand should write the script; push back on this. The most effective influencer content sounds like the creator, not the brand. The disclosure box was rewritten this year to match ASA New Zealand's actual current guidance more precisely; the old "#ad or #sponsored is the standard" line understated the requirement. ASA's real standard, from its Guidance Note on Identification of Advertisements, is that it must be obvious to consumers that content is an ad at their first interaction with it, they should not have to click through, read a bio, or interact further to work it out. A disclosure buried in a bio or revealed only after engagement is explicitly not sufficient. Payment does not have to be cash: gifted products, free travel, event tickets, or other value in kind all count. Recommended labels are "Ad," "Advert," "Advertisement," or "Sponsored content," rather than softer phrasing like "in collaboration with" or "thanks to," which does not clearly signal a commercial relationship. Ask students: "Have you ever seen an influencer post that you suspected was sponsored but not labelled? What did that do to your perception of the brand?"
Influencer marketing

Measuring campaign performance

Frame by the goal first: KPIs follow from goals, not the other way around.
Awareness campaigns
Reach · Impressions · Follower growth · Share of voice · Earned media value
Engagement campaigns
Likes · Comments · Saves · Shares · Engagement rate
Conversion campaigns
Click-through rate · Website traffic (UTM-tracked) · Promo code redemptions · Cost per acquisition
Key principle: Evaluation KPIs must be set before the campaign launches, not after. You need a baseline to measure against.
The goal-first framing is the most important structural point on this slide. Students often arrive at measurement by listing every metric they can think of. The correct sequence is: define the goal first, then select the KPIs that measure progress toward that goal. An awareness campaign measured by conversion rate is a category error. A conversion campaign measured by impressions is equally misaligned. The UTM tracking point is practical and worth flagging: without UTM parameters on the link in the influencer's bio or post, website traffic from the campaign is invisible in Google Analytics. This is a common gap in influencer campaigns run by small or early-stage businesses. Earned media value (EMV) is a disputed metric: it has value as a rough proxy but should not be reported uncritically. If students use it in their audit work, they should be able to explain how it is calculated and what its limitations are.

Apply the framework to Music Planet

Your task, in four steps:
Decide your context, choose a tier and audience segment, identify two or three real candidate influencers and check what tier they actually fall into, then apply the selection KPIs from Slide 6.
1. Decide your context, before you prompt anything ~2 min
Write down what you would put in the context part of your prompt: which Music Planet audience segment you're targeting, what the campaign is trying to achieve, and anything else the model needs to avoid a generic answer. Be ready to say why each piece of context matters.
2. Choose a tier and audience segment ~1 min
Based on that context, choose an influencer tier (nano, micro, macro, mega) and the specific Music Planet audience segment you're targeting: students, hobbyists, gigging musicians, or another group.
3. Identify two or three real candidates ~4–5 min
Search for actual NZ-based creators. Hashtags for NZ music and gear demos, or YouTube instrument-review channels, are a good starting point. For each candidate, note their real follower count and which tier it actually places them in. Does it match the tier you chose in Step 2? Verify every account is real before you write it down: if your LLM suggested a name, check it exists rather than taking an AI-generated creator on trust.
4. Apply your selection KPIs ~2 min
Pick two or three selection KPIs from Slide 6. For each candidate, say what you'd need to see to be confident in a partnership, and what you still don't know from a follower count alone.
Share your context, your candidates, and your KPI reasoning with the group before we move to Section 2. The analytical habits here (context first, verify before you trust, evidence over assumption) carry directly into the Rival IQ session.
The slide now shows a time budget per step (roughly 2 / 1 / 4–5 / 2 minutes, totalling 8–10) so students can pace themselves without checking in constantly, but treat it as a guide, not a hard stop, especially on Step 3 where searching can run long. Give students the first two minutes for Step 1 alone before they touch a search engine or an LLM: the point is to make them commit to their context and reasoning in writing first, so they can't quietly reverse-engineer it after seeing candidates. This is a deliberate callback to the CRAFT framework and to the Week 2 material on giving AI enough context to avoid a generic answer: ask a student to read their Step 1 context aloud before moving on, and note how much more specific the good answers are than "Music Planet wants an influencer." For Steps 2 and 3, the intended finding is that nano or micro influencers in the local music, tuition, or gear-demonstration space are likely to fit Music Planet best, primarily because of audience fit and engagement rate. Music Planet's value proposition rests on expertise and trust built over 40-plus years; it works best when the audience already identifies with the influencer as a genuine musician, not just a promoter. A gigging guitarist or music teacher who demonstrates gear and technique is a better fit than a lifestyle creator with a much larger following. The tier-check in Step 3 is deliberately built in as a discernment exercise: students often assume an account "feels" nano or micro without checking, and a genuine surprise, an account that has quietly grown into the next tier up, or one whose follower count doesn't match its apparent engagement, is a good discussion point. The instruction to verify every account is real is not procedural box-ticking: LLMs asked to suggest influencers in a specific niche will sometimes produce a plausible-sounding name that does not exist, or misattribute a real handle to the wrong niche. Treat any student who catches this as a live demonstration of exactly the verification habit from the Week 2 hallucination discussion, and flag it to the group. For KPIs in Step 4: engagement rate and audience demographics (specifically NZ-based musicians and music hobbyists, by instrument or genre where possible) should rank highest. Follower quality is also worth prioritising given the prevalence of bought followers in some niches. Use the debrief to bridge into Section 2: "Now that we have a framework for thinking about influencer selection and performance, let's apply that same analytical rigour to Music Planet's broader social media presence using Rival IQ."

Assessing social media presence

Frequency, consistency, and volume of posting
Overview Dashboard
Start here for a high-level view of posting activity. Look for automated insights on audience, activity (posts per week or month), and trends compared to competitors. Bar charts show Music Planet's position relative to the landscape average.
Leaderboard
Ranks posting frequency across your competitive landscape. Use the Posts/Month line charts to identify whether Music Planet is over- or under-posting relative to Rockshop and MusicWorks. This is the cadence calibration view.
Social Posts
Drill into post-level data for timing patterns, frequency trends, and top and bottom performers. Use this to identify whether Music Planet's posting schedule is consistent or irregular.
What to record in your audit template: Average posts per week or month across platforms · How Music Planet's posting frequency compares to each competitor · Whether the posting pattern is consistent or showing gaps
The Overview Dashboard is the right starting point for students who are new to Rival IQ. It surfaces automated insights that give a useful orientation before diving into the detail. The Leaderboard is where the competitive comparison becomes concrete: students can see at a glance whether Music Planet is posting more or less than its competitors and whether that frequency correlates with higher or lower engagement. The key analytical question here is not "how often does Music Planet post?" but "is Music Planet's posting cadence appropriate given its audience and competitive context?" A brand posting twice a week might be under-investing relative to competitors, or it might be posting at a quality-over-quantity level that serves it well. The data surfaces the pattern; the student supplies the interpretation. Remind students: frequency data without context is not analysis. Connecting frequency to engagement outcomes is where the analytical value is.
Social media audit

Evaluating engagement metrics

Likes, shares, comments, and overall interaction rates
Overview Dashboard
Breaks down engagement by section. Look at dynamic charts for engagement rate, likes, comments, shares, and reactions. These are normalised for fair cross-brand comparison.
Leaderboard
Ranks engagement metrics with competitor averages and time-series line charts. Compare Music Planet's likes, shares, and comments relative to audience size against Rockshop and MusicWorks.
Social Posts
Reveals per-post engagement breakdowns. Use post tags to categorise high-interaction content and identify what content types are driving Music Planet's best-performing posts.
Engagement rate formula: (Total Engagement / Posts) / Followers; the public data calculation Rival IQ uses for fair comparison across accounts of different sizes.
What to record: Music Planet's average engagement rate by platform · How it benchmarks against each competitor · Which post types drive the highest engagement for Music Planet
Engagement rate is the most important normalising metric in social media analytics. Raw like counts are misleading because they scale with audience size. Engagement rate removes the size effect and enables genuine comparison between accounts. Students often misread high follower counts as a sign of strong performance; push them to look at rate, not volume. A pattern worth watching for with retail competitors: a larger, more generalist competitor can carry a bigger audience but a comparatively low engagement rate if its content leans toward catalogue or product-announcement posts, while a more specialist competitor can post less often and still show a stronger rate because its audience is more self-selected. Students should check whether that pattern actually holds in their own Rival IQ data rather than assuming it; this is a hypothesis to test, not a given. If it does appear, it is a useful teaching moment: size does not equal effectiveness. When reviewing per-post data, students should look for patterns in content type, not just the top performers in isolation. One viral post can skew averages significantly.

Mentions, sentiment, and brand conversation

Use to find user creators in Music Planet's niche: musicians, music educators, and gear reviewers active in the New Zealand market.
What to assess
Three areas of focus
Volume and sentiment of brand mentions · Whether UGC content is high quality and relevant to Music Planet's audience · Whether Music Planet is actively engaging with user content or ignoring it
UGC analysis depends on whether Music Planet has a meaningful volume of organic brand mentions. If mention volume is low, this is itself a finding about brand conversation and community health.
Social listening is the most often underused feature in Rival IQ for student audits. Many students focus entirely on the brand's own posts and ignore what users are saying about the brand organically. This section asks students to reverse that perspective: what is the brand's community saying, and is the brand listening? For Music Planet, the relevant conversation is likely concentrated among gigging musicians, students, parents buying instruments for their children, and hobbyists. The New Zealand music community is active on Instagram and Facebook, and to a lesser extent TikTok and Reddit. If Social Listening surfaces negative sentiment around pricing, stock availability, or in-store or online customer service, that is strategically important information for the audit. Conversely, positive UGC from satisfied customers is a potential influencer or ambassador pipeline; connecting this to the influencer marketing framework from Section 1 is a useful synthesis move. Low mention volume is a legitimate finding: it may indicate that Music Planet's audience is not a social-sharing community, or that the brand has not yet built enough awareness to generate organic conversation.

The competitor comparison is the most strategically valuable component of the social media audit. The two-competitor structure is useful precisely because Rockshop and MusicWorks are both direct, like-for-like NZ music retailers, so any gap in posting cadence or engagement rate is a genuine strategic signal rather than an artefact of comparing across different industries. If one competitor's engagement rate clearly outperforms the other, that is worth investigating for what is actually driving the difference, content type, posting consistency, or audience niche. Running the Rival IQ analysis

Your task:
Work through the four analysis areas in sequence using Rival IQ. For each area, record your findings directly in your audit template. Use your preferred LLM alongside Rival IQ to help interpret the data, but apply your own critical judgement before accepting any AI-generated interpretation.
The four areas
  1. Social media presence: frequency and consistency across platforms
  2. Engagement metrics: rates and patterns against competitors
  3. UGC and social listening: mention volume, sentiment, and community health
  4. Social media strategy: what is Music Planet doing well, and where are the gaps?
Critical review
  • Are the AI findings specific to Music Planet's context, or generic social media observations?
  • Does the LLM identify the competitor most similar to Music Planet's strategic position?
  • Where does the output require your own judgement to make it useful?
Build a prompt that provides context about Music Planet, the competitor landscape, and the specific Rival IQ data. Ask for strategic interpretation and a summary table of findings by area. Share your prompt in the debrief and compare outputs.
Give students 35–40 minutes for this activity. Circulate actively: the most common failure mode is students recording data from Rival IQ without interpreting what it means for Music Planet's strategy. A finding like "Music Planet posts 3.2 times per week on Instagram" is not useful in an audit without the comparative context (is that above or below the landscape average?) and the strategic implication (should Music Planet post more, less, or differently?). For students who are struggling with interpretation, a useful prompt is: "What would you tell Music Planet's marketing manager to do differently based on this data?" That forces a practical, action-oriented framing that tends to surface better analysis than purely descriptive approaches. The LLM prompt guidance in the debrief is intentionally minimal; students should construct the prompt themselves rather than following a template. This is a prompt engineering exercise as much as a social media audit exercise. In the debrief, ask two or three students to share their prompts and compare the quality of the outputs they received.

The SEO section of the audit template has four parts, and it is worth understanding what each is actually asking for before opening Semrush. On-page SEO covers everything within Music Planet's own pages: are the keywords realistic for what people search when buying instruments or gear, do title tags and meta descriptions target those keywords, are headers used sensibly, are URLs clean, is internal linking sensible, and are images given alt text. Technical SEO is broader than it might sound: beyond mobile responsiveness and page speed, it now also covers crawlability (can Google's crawler find and index the site via the sitemap and robots.txt, and are there broken links or redirect chains), duplicate content and canonicalisation (are near-duplicate pages accidentally competing with each other), and structured data (is schema markup present and correct, which affects whether Music Planet's products can appear as rich results in search). Off-page SEO measures Music Planet's authority beyond its own site: backlink quality and quantity via Backlink Analytics, toxic or spammy links via Backlink Audit, an overall Authority Score (Semrush's own 0 to 100 metric, not the same thing as Domain Authority from Moz or Domain Rating from Ahrefs), and general online reputation. Competitor analysis compares all of this against Rockshop and MusicWorks specifically, using the Keyword Gap tool to see which keywords are shared across all three sites, which only competitors rank for, and which are unique to Music Planet. The through-line for the whole section: the template wants analysis and interpretation, not a list of numbers. Stating a metric is the first step; explaining what it means for Music Planet's ability to be found is the actual task.

Section 2 — 15 minutes

Website overview

Evaluating musicplanet.co.nz across nine audit criteria, combining AI-assisted analysis with mandatory manual review.
Section 2 evaluates musicplanet.co.nz against the nine criteria in the audit template, using a two-part approach: an AI-assisted pass using Manus, an AI agent that can browse the live site, followed by a mandatory manual review of the parts no automated tool can judge reliably. The reason the manual step cannot be skipped is straightforward: things like whether a cookie banner is genuinely transparent, whether the Privacy Policy and Terms of Service are easy to find and written in plain language, and whether accessibility support is a genuine effort or a checkbox, all require a human visitor exercising judgement, not a tool pattern-matching against a checklist. Before the activity, make sure you understand what the MetaPrompt is: a tool that turns a plain description of a task into a well-structured, CRAFT-based prompt, which you then run in Manus.

Walk through the nine criteria as a group before running any tool, since several overlap in what you are actually looking at on the page. UI/UX evaluation, navigation, design and layout, CTAs, interactivity, search functionality, and social media integration are largely observable and can be assessed reasonably well by an AI agent browsing the site. Accessibility is assessed against WCAG 2.2 Level AA, which is the current W3C standard as of mid-2026; WCAG 3.0 exists only as a working draft and should not be used as a benchmark, and it is worth checking this is still accurate closer to when you actually run this assessment, since standards bodies do update these designations. Design and Layout also asks whether the site looks current and well-maintained: no outdated copyright dates, broken content, or stale sections. Criterion 9, ethical and legal considerations, is marked manual-only because no automated tool reliably judges whether cookie consent is meaningful, whether a Privacy Policy and Terms of Service are both present and easy to find, or whether data collection through sign-ups and quizzes is disclosed honestly; these need a human reading the actual page. Two further checks, trust and credibility signals (HTTPS, testimonials, an accessible About Us page, clear contact details) and value proposition clarity (can a first-time visitor tell what Music Planet offers within the first few seconds on the homepage), are also best judged by a human visitor rather than an AI agent, and are covered on the manual review slide alongside criterion 9, even though they are not numbered separately in the nine-criteria grid. The red highlight on criterion 9 is intentional.

Section 3 — 20 minutes

Traffic analytics audit

Three business goals, a goal-to-KPI-to-benchmark table you complete yourself, a separate site-health check, and strategic insights, all read against what Music Planet is trying to achieve.
Section 3 restructures the traffic analytics audit around Music Planet's actual business goals rather than working through a fixed list of GA4 reports. The three goals, taken from Music Planet's marketing brief, are: increase brand awareness, increase engagement, and drive online sales. For each goal, you select the GA4 KPI or KPIs that would genuinely move if that goal were being achieved, and identify a benchmark to judge performance against. This is genuinely your task, the deck gives you the process and the goals, not the KPI answers. Separately, you identify two or three general site-health KPIs that reflect overall site condition independent of any single goal, again your choice to make and justify. This structure matters because it forces every metric you report to answer a real question: does this number tell us something about whether Music Planet is achieving what it is actually trying to achieve, rather than just being a number that happened to be easy to pull from GA4. If you were not given the GA4 exports for this exercise, that needs to be resolved before you can complete the activities in this section; note that students work from provided exports rather than live GA4 access, since you do not have login credentials for Music Planet's actual account.

Section 4 — 20 minutes

Paid advertising

A three-step assessment: identify paid activity in the GA4 channel report, assess Paid Search, then assess Paid Social using both GA4 and the Meta Ad Library.
Section 4 has a different shape from previous years: it is now a structured three-step process rather than a single Meta Ad Library review. Step 1 works out what paid activity actually exists, using the GA4 channel report, including a channel called Cross-network that is easy to miss because the word "Paid" does not appear in its name. Step 2 assesses Paid Search, which is assessed entirely from GA4 because Google, unlike Meta, does not offer a public ad-transparency tool. Step 3 assesses Paid Social using both GA4, for actual traffic and outcomes, and the Meta Ad Library, for the creative strategy behind those numbers, because the two sources answer genuinely different questions and neither one alone gives the full picture. The Meta Ad Library itself is free and needs no login; search "Music Planet" with the country filter set to New Zealand, and confirm you have found the advertiser tied to musicplanet.co.nz, since more than one result may appear under a similar name. If Music Planet has no currently active ads when you check, that absence is itself a finding worth recording, and the All ads filter will show whether there has been historical activity even if nothing is running right now.

This slide uses the LLM as a reviewer, not as the original analyst. Students should already have two pieces of evidence: the Paid Social GA4 table from Slide 19 and the four-lens Meta Ad Library observation table from Slide 20. They should paste both into the same chat and ask the model to stress-test the interpretation. The model's job is to look for weak reasoning, unsupported claims, contradictions, missing caveats, and places where the evidence does not support the conclusion.

A good stress-test prompt should ask the LLM to do five things. First, identify any claim that is not directly supported by either a GA4 value or a specific ad observation. Second, identify any overclaim, such as treating ad longevity as proof of campaign profitability. Third, identify contradictions, such as saying paid social is conversion-focused when the ads are mostly awareness-led or when GA4 shows no Key Events. Fourth, identify missing caveats, especially around lack of cost, CPC, ROI, or ROAS data. Fifth, draft a tighter synthesis paragraph that clearly separates evidence, interpretation, and limitation.

Students should not accept the LLM's revised paragraph automatically. They should use it as a quality-control step. If the LLM adds a claim that was not in the evidence, cut it. If it softens a claim appropriately, keep the caveat. If it suggests a better structure, use that structure but check every statement. The central rule is evidence traceability: every sentence in the final paid social write-up should be traceable either to a GA4 metric, a specific observation from the Ad Library, or a clearly stated limitation.

Expected output from this slide: a revised paid social synthesis paragraph and a short list of claims that were cut, softened, or strengthened after the stress test. This is especially useful for students who missed the workshop because it gives them a practical self-check before putting the section into the final audit.

This final slide should help students who missed the workshop understand what they should have produced by the end of the deck. The goal is not simply to have completed four disconnected activities. A useful way to self-check the whole audit is to ask three questions. First, what is the main story the evidence tells about Music Planet's digital presence? Second, which sections support that story, and do any sections contradict it? Third, what evidence is still missing or limited? For example, SEO might show a visibility issue for non-branded terms, Traffic Analytics might show reliance on existing visitors, and Paid Advertising might show whether paid channels are compensating for that acquisition gap. Students should also make sure their use of AI is visible as a method, not hidden as an answer generator. They should note where they used an LLM to identify reports, select KPIs, interpret exports, or stress-test a conclusion; and they should note where they manually verified or corrected the model. These moments are useful for the methodology reflection because they show judgement. The strongest audits use AI to accelerate the work while still making the student responsible for evidence selection, verification, and final interpretation.
